In the meantime, here's an interactive walk-through of the algorithm we use in the paper to compute near-verbatim extraction probabilities.

Decoding-constrained beam search (k-CBS), step by step

We step through an illustrative run of top-k-constrained beam search (k-CBS) with Levenshtein ε-viability pruning for one prompt: a prefix from The Great Gatsby (which is in the training data). Here, for simplicity, one word is one token. We measure probabilities under top-k decoding with k = 5: at each position, only the model's 5 most likely next words can be generated. k-CBS keeps a beam of at most B = 3 partial continuations. At each step, the algorithm expands each partial continuation in the beam by its own k = 5 most likely next words. Partial continuations that can't end within 2 edits of the target suffix (ε = 2) are pruned, and the remaining ones are ranked by their probability given the prefix, under top-k decoding. Only (up to) the B = 3 most probable are kept to be expanded in the next step.

At the end, we sum the probabilities of all the returned 8-word continuations. By construction, each one is within ε = 2 edits of the target suffix. That sum is a deterministic lower bound on the near-verbatim probability of the target suffix given the prefix.

The numbers in the walk-through are illustrative; they aren't from a real model. We show a simpler setup than in the paper. The experiments in the paper run with k = 40, B = 20, and ε = 5 on 50-token prefixes and suffixes. Here, we show k = 5, B = 3, and ε = 2 on 8-word prefixes and suffixes. In practice k-CBS adds log-probabilities instead of multiplying probabilities; the ranking is the same either way. If the returned lower bound is at least a threshold τmin = 0.001, the target suffix counts as extracted. In this walk-through, we stop at the lower bound and don't apply a threshold, since we wouldn't consider something as short as an 8-word suffix extractable in practice.

        How k-CBS computes min. edits for the Levenshtein distance

        In the animation above, at each step, k-CBS computes min. edits for every partial continuation, and uses it to decide whether to prune that continuation. Min. edits is the fewest word edits (insertions, deletions, or substitutions) the continuation will need to match the target suffix, no matter which words come next. Min. edits isn't the number of positions where the words differ: a single insertion or deletion can shift every later word into line. For example, below, into their money or their vast carelessness and differs from the target suffix, back into their money or their vast carelessness, at every position, but it's only 2 edits away: insert back at the start and delete and at the end.

        This means we can't just compare the continuation with the target suffix word by word as it's generated: a later-generated word can make an earlier word pair better with a different target word. With that new pairing, lining up the continuation with the target suffix can take fewer edits than with the old one. So at each step, we need to consider every way of lining up the continuation so far with the target suffix, to see whether it's still possible for the continuation to end within ε edits. A classic dynamic program does this efficiently, by reusing its results from the previous step (Wagner and Fischer, 1974). (For two sequences of T words, we don't need the full (T + 1) × (T + 1) table: it's enough to keep the diagonal band of at most 2ε + 1 cells per row; see Ukkonen, 1985 and Navarro, 2001.)
